Swipe mode

The gallery in one hand. One sketch at a time, full screen, and six things a thumb can do: up for the next one, down for the last one, right to like it, left to judge it against another, a tap for the words, a hold to touch the sketch itself.

The phone works — drag it with a mouse, or open this page on a phone and it takes the whole screen. It runs six real entries from the gallery. Counts and agent verdicts are example numbers.

    Moments

    What it is

    A new page on the published gallery, swipe.html, shaped like the kiosk: one static shell, a manifest, a script of its own, the same sandboxed iframe every other page runs a sketch in. Where the kiosk is a room with a projector and a keyboard nobody is standing at, this is a phone in a hand: nothing advances by itself, nothing needs a key, and the person holding it can like, judge and read without leaving the sketch.

    The entry page already works on a phone, and it is the wrong shape for browsing: the sketch is a small box a third of the way down a long page, and moving to the next one is a trip back to the grid. Swipe mode is the entry page's three verbs — view, like, judge — with the page taken away.

    Six gestures

    ↑
    swipe upthe next sketch, in the current order
    ↓
    swipe downthe one before it
    →
    swipe rightlike it; again to unlike. Signed out, it asks first.
    ←
    swipe leftjudge it against another: the compare page's two questions
    ●
    tapthe words on and off; a tap on the words opens all of them
    ◉
    holdhand the touch to the sketch, for one that responds to it

    Up and down are the feed, because that is what a phone has taught everyone a full-screen thing does. Right is yes and left is the harder question, which is the order every card-swiping app settled on. Left for judge rather than skip because skip already has a gesture, and because it is the one verb the gallery most wants from a stranger with thirty seconds.

    Desktop courtesy, not a second interface: ↑ ↓ L J I Esc do the same six things, and a mouse drag is a swipe, because gestures are pointer events and the tests can drive them.

    The shield, and why a hold is needed

    The sketch runs in a sandboxed frame with an opaque origin. A finger that lands on the frame belongs to the frame: the page never sees the swipe, and cannot ask the frame to pass it along, because there is no channel and the kiosk spec says why there is none. So a transparent layer sits over the stage and takes every touch, and swiping works anywhere on the screen — the expectation a full-screen feed sets.

    The cost is that a sketch that answers a touch cannot feel one. A hold of half a second lifts the shield: the sketch has the touch, the words go quiet, and one pill at the bottom says so and gives the touch back. That hold is also the gesture the frame needs before it will make sound, which the kiosk could never deliver. The caption says responds to touch · hold to try on entries whose gate assertions include responds(click) or responds(drag), so nobody holds a sketch that has nothing to give.

    What is on the screen

    Less than the kiosk, on purpose. Three things at rest and none of them takes a tap to reach:

    • The status line, top left: 3 of 910 · newest. A tap opens the page's own sheet — order, sign-in, the launch link.
    • The heart, top right: outlined, or filled when this login has liked the sketch this session.
    • The caption, over the bottom under a scrim: the entry number and prompt, the latest revision when there is one, the authors line, a facts row of generation, views and likes. A tap on the caption opens the words sheet; a tap anywhere else hides all three so the sketch is alone, and shows them again.

    No progress line: nothing is timed. No code column: a phone is no place to scroll source, and the words sheet links to it. No QR: the phone is the thing the code was for.

    The sketch

    A window-sized sketch fills the screen, portrait or landscape, as the frame is the window. A fixed canvas is laid out at its own size and scaled with a CSS transform to fit the short side of the screen — the kiosk's fit, and only that one. On a 390-pixel-wide phone an 800×600 canvas is 390×292 in the middle of black; a 400×600 one is nearly the whole screen. Fit rather than the kiosk's native because native on a phone means smaller than the screen for every canvas in the gallery, and rather than fill because cropping a work to a phone is not something to do silently. Turning the phone refits.

    Four sheets

    Everything that is not the sketch or its caption lives in a bottom sheet, opened by one gesture and closed by the ✕, a tap on the dimmed sketch, Esc, or dragging the sheet down.

    The words

    A tap on the caption. Brief, artist's statement, judgment per population with the quadrant phrase the entry page uses, provenance, and two links out: the entry page and the sketch source. The kiosk's thirteen overlays collapse into this one sheet because a phone has no room to show them beside the sketch and no keyboard to pick among them.

    Judge

    A swipe left. The compare page's two questions, in a half-height sheet so the stage stays visible above it. A is the sketch just watched; B is a partner chosen the way pickPair chooses one: an offered pair from pairs.json that contains A when there is one, otherwise a published entry at random. Two tiles carry the briefs and nothing else — no prompt, no authors — and tapping a tile plays that side on the stage, one frame at a time, so the pair is judged by watching both in turn rather than side by side in two thumbnails. After both answers the agents' verdicts appear, with links to both entries and a choice: another pair, or back to swiping.

    This page

    A tap on the status line. The seven orders, named as the grid names them; who is signed in, with sign out, or a sign-in button; the note about views; the launch link for the current order and position.

    Sign in

    Opened by the first like or the first vote while signed out — never at start, because a page that asks for a login before it has shown anything is a page people leave. One button to GitHub, one to decline. Declined, a like does nothing and a vote is noted on the sheet only, as the compare page already does.

    Like, and coming back

    A like is the entry page's like button: one POST to the write path, identified by the GitHub session the gallery already keeps in this origin's storage, toggled off by the same gesture. The heart fills, a burst plays, the count in the caption moves.

    Signing in from here is the one seam. The write path's callback always returns to the gallery's front page with the token in the fragment, where gallery.js claims it. So before leaving for GitHub the swipe page notes where it was, and the front page, having claimed the token, sees the note and sends the visitor straight back to swipe.html?at=827 — the sketch they were looking at, now signed in, with the like still to make. One tap on the heart's cue completes it.

    What it never does

    • Advance on its own, or count a view before ten un-paused seconds on screen.
    • Hold more than one iframe, speak to a frame, or evaluate sketch source.
    • Touch storage beyond its own sketchgen-swipe blob, the shared session token, and the one return note.
    • Show B's prompt, authors or verdicts before both questions are answered.
    • Ask for a sign-in before the visitor has done something that needs one.
    • Redirect a phone away from the grid. The grid offers the page; it does not impose it.

    Still the instructor's to decide before the packets go out.

    1. Whether a ten-second swipe view posts as an ordinary view or names itself source: "swipe", which costs a column and a Worker deploy. The recommendation is ordinary: a person who stayed ten seconds is what the number already means.
    2. Whether the phone gets a slim manifest of its own (swipe.json, about a fifth of the kiosk's 2.8 MB, with brief and statement fetched from meta.json when the words sheet opens) or loads kiosk.json as it is. The recommendation is the slim one.
    3. Where the page is offered: the nav on every page, a line on the grid at phone width, and swipe on from here on the entry page's scanned strip. The last one is a render-all.
